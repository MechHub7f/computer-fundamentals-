### Financial method (S01–S04)

For each company, fix the purchase date, sale date, nominal holding, currency and treatment of dividends. For dividends held in cash without interest, total return is `(sale proceeds + entitled dividends − purchase cost) / purchase cost`. Both price and dividend must refer to the same nominal holding. Convert currencies consistently for a common investor, specifying whether each dividend is converted on receipt or held in the original currency. Fees, taxes, exchange spreads, trading venue, dates, capital changes and dividend entitlements may affect the result. If profits are reinvested, the reinvestment price and extra shares must be tracked at each date. Compare exits after allowing for any additional dividends as well as the sale price. A sample of annual closing prices can identify only the best *observed annual closing date*, not a daily optimum.

Petram (S03), printed p. 19 and note 8, says the VOC had no single fixed denomination for one share and that market values were expressed as percentages of nominal value; ƒ3,000 became a common trading unit, not a universal minimum. The glossary at pp. 206–208 explains nominal value and cash dividend conventions. A dividend of 20% of nominal value is not a 20% return on the much higher market purchase price. The dissertation's early-period return calculations do not cover this assignment's dates.

Golez and Koudijs (S01), appendix pp. 2–5, identify Van Dillen's transcription of Amsterdam newspaper quotations from 1723 and Neal's transcription of London quotations from 1698 as sources, and identify separate dividend sources. The authors discuss exchange-rate conversion and corporate adjustments around pp. 6–7. Their downloadable public workbook is an aggregate index, according to a research-agent inspection, and should not be represented as separate VOC and EIC price series. The appendix is a roadmap, not proof of particular 1725 or 1750 quotations.

Beach, Norman, and Wills (S02), pp. 62–63 and 69–77, warn about transcription errors, ex-dividend dates and distinguishing spot from time/forward quotations. Their article discusses additional Amsterdam observations for 1724–1727 and changing market conventions for British securities, especially around 1747. For 1724–1725 it identifies EIC ex-dividend quotation changes in mid-January and July (figure 7, p. 71): a January purchase's precise date matters. Do not automatically extend their finding about British securities quoted in Amsterdam to VOC shares.

Klerk de Reus (S04), appendix VI, compiles VOC dividends from directors' resolutions. The downloaded OCR is a useful lead, but tabular alignment cannot yet be trusted: the 146 MB page-image PDF is available from the Leiden item page, not downloaded in this round. A research agent's preliminary reading suggests amounts for 1725–26 and 1745–50, but none should enter a calculation until the rows, payment dates and column headings are checked on page images. This retrospective 1894 compilation is not itself an eighteenth-century primary publication.

## Contemporary primary evidence and limits

**P01 — Dutch descriptive history, 1739, printed p. 472.** Extracted OCR says settlers on Banda have the land cultivated “door hunne Slaaven” and deliver nutmeg and mace to company officials. Tentative translation: “by their enslaved people”; the larger sentence connects plantation production with Company delivery. This establishes a contemporary author's description, not an independently audited inventory of every plantation. The description predates 1740 and cannot report the Batavia massacre. OCR shows line breaks and early spelling; check the scan before quoting. Printed pp. 448–451 also discuss commercial arrangements, but conjectural totals should not be mistaken for financial accounts.

**P02 — Batavia amnesty of 22 October 1740 and directors' 1742 letter in an 1887 edition, printed pp. 510–514.** A research agent located the amnesty's reference on p. 511 to the destruction of Chinese people in and outside the city; the edict is an interested official justification, not an impartial reconstruction. At p. 513, a 1742 Heren XVII letter criticizes particular summary executions as closer to murder than justice. The extracted OCR contains “moorden, dan na regt te doen.” Check original-language context and scan, especially which acts the criticism addresses. Cite the period document and its later edition separately from the nineteenth-century editor's explanation. The two documents in this collection are distinct primary texts, but consult both directly before counting them separately in a bibliography.

**P03 — 1726 English charter, reproduced in Shaw, printed pp. 230–248.** The extract at p. 230 presents Company settlements as benefiting from a “strict and equal Distribution of Justice”; this is charter rhetoric, not proof of equal justice in practice. The following corporate provisions establish mayoral and aldermanic structures, including requirements relating to natural-born subjects. Verify the exact city and wording against pp. 231 onward; a similar provision also appears for Bombay later in the document. This charter postdates the purchase date, so describe it as a subsequent development rather than a 1725 condition.

**P04 — shareholder pamphlet, 1749, printed pp. 11–12.** The author discusses £4.2 million in advances by the Company for public service and annuities of £158,000 a year, subject to terms of repayment; the same passage separates £3.2 million already advanced from £1 million “now to be advanced.” Therefore do not present the full £4.2 million as already paid without checking the context and subsequent outcome. It concerns the Company's claim on government and public credit, not what the Company borrowed from government or its shareholder dividend. The author is an interested director arguing to proprietors. The date in the title needs Old Style calendar analysis.

**P05 — St Helena correspondence and consultations, excerpts dated 1725, 1727 and 1739, edited 1885, printed pp. 169, 182.** OCR p. 169 reports an instruction that a Black person freed by an owner for good conduct must leave on the first ship; the adjacent 1727 entry reports spending on provisions for the Company's Black workers and proposes saving money by employing them to fish. OCR p. 182 identifies nine Company-owned enslaved people, including men, women and children brought from Bombay, and describes their escape. This is direct evidence that EIC operations included ownership of enslaved people and restrictions on freedom, but the edition is abridged and may omit context; check the printed text, dates and surrounding entries. Do not attribute Janisch's editorial commentary to Company officials.

**P06 — Amsterdam newspaper, 7 January 1730, pp. 1–2.** A proofread Wikisource transcription reports the Ridderkerk, charged to the Amsterdam chamber, departing Batavia 19 July 1729 and arriving at Texel 4 January 1730, with pepper, sapanwood and two kinds of tea listed. The local HTML contains the transcription; the facsimile has not been checked. It shows the nature of a reported cargo and long-distance shipping, not sale revenue, shareholder dividends, or a VOC stock quotation. This Dutch newspaper and P01 provide two separate non-English primary-source candidates.

## Modern contextual analysis

**S05 — Mentz on Madras, pp. 37–38 and 48–50.** The article explains the French capture of Madras in September 1746, internal conflicts and private trade, and notes its return under the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. It also investigates differing interests of Company servants, private merchants, shareholders and directors. This offers relevant context on military and commercial risk, but does not establish how EIC shares responded on a particular day or whether 1748 was an optimal sale year. Check the printed pages and author's qualifications before incorporating it.

## Ethical framework and open questions

Both companies can be connected to slavery by relevant contemporary texts (P01 and P05); P02 addresses VOC government violence. Do not assume that one terrible event establishes a defensible quantitative ranking of the two companies, or that similar categories imply identical severity. Distinguish the impact of holding or buying existing shares from financing a new issue; the ethical question could involve complicity, profit from coerced labour, institutional power, or support for future operations. Consider the option of declining either investment separately from the forced-choice question. A forced-choice recommendation requires a clearly stated standard and comparable evidence, not an unsupported declaration that one company was ethical.
