An Ancestor’s Investment: Britain and the VOC  
This is an informal alternate history. so, The Dutch figures have been constructed using annual averages to represent the price paid when conducting the trade. They have been assumed to pay a nominal capital dividend. The historical context has been taken from the sources given.

Decisions  
I would advise the ancestor to invest in the British East India Company and sell in 1750. This is because the British investment produces the greatest return once dividends are included. However, if you do not wish to gain any profits from slavery and forced colonial practices, then I would advise against investing in either company. Neither of the companies is ethically clean.

Differences between the Companies  
Both companies involved investing in businesses with political privileges. The VOC had six Dutch chambers working together under the Heeren XVII. It had Batavia as its administration centre in Asia. The VOC traded in spices, connecting Europe with Asia. An example of this can be seen in a newspaper from Amsterdam. The Ridderkerk returned in January 1730 carrying pepper, sapanwood, green tea and bohea tea for the Amsterdam chamber. 

The British also had political powers in their settlements. The charter granted by George I in 1726 gave the settlements of Madras, Bombay and Calcutta their own municipalities and judicial systems. Whilst the charter begins by praising how there would be a ‘strict and equal Distribution of Justice’, this does not mean that the colonists would have been treated equally. both of the companies had wide spread polical power.

The EIC also had involvement in British public finances. In a pamphlet written by an anonymous director for the shareholders, government annuities and advances of £4.2 million are mentioned. However, it explains how £3.2 million had already been advanced and how a further £1 million was to be advanced. This shows that there were plans to pay certain amounts in this way. It does not show that the full amount had been paid or how much the shareholders would have received in dividends.

Risks during the Investment  
There were risks to the investment due to political factors. Søren Mentz explains how the French took Madras in September 1746. However, it was returned in the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. Mentz goes into detail about the conflicting interests between Company servants, private traders, directors and shareholders. Just because a private trader was doing well did not mean that all shareholders would be. Even though the military had recovered Madras, this does not mean that it would have been the best time to sell.

Looking at the Dutch figures, there was a large drop in price. The average price per year of the VOC in Amsterdam was 642.58$ in 1725. This dropped to 449.23$ in 1745 and further to 404.29$ in 1747. However, by 1750 it had increased to 557.39. Overall, the final average price was still lower than the original. This means that just because it was a well-known and long-lasting trading company, someone investing at a high price would not necessarily have gained capital growth.

However, there were dividends. Klerk de Reus has compiled a list of dividends in his retrospective work. There is a schedule showing when the resolution and payment were made. From the data given in the annual dataset, we cannot determine whether the investor would have received the payment for that year.

Comparison of Returns  
The VOC price should not be confused with the dividend yield. Petram explains how traders would quote the price as a percentage of the nominal capital. There is no standard unit for one share. Therefore, if you paid 20% of the nominal capital, this would not mean that you received a 20% yield.

We have assumed that 100 units of nominal capital have been invested. This means that in Britain, 150 units would have been paid for the shares. Each year from 1726 to 1750, you would receive 8 units in dividends. In 1750, you would sell for 200 units. This means that you would have received 200 units in total from the dividends. The final wealth would be 400 units. This means that you would have made a profit of 250 units. This is a total return of 166.7%.

For the VOC, the initial proxy would be 642.58. In 1750, the proxy would be 557.39. The dividends can be calculated by adding up each year's dividend rate in the dataset. This gives a total of 490 units from the 100 nominal units. The final wealth would be 1047.39. This means that there would be a profit of around 404.81. This is a return of 63.0%. Due to the dividends, there is a positive total return. The ‘Dividendspayable’ column on the balance sheet has not been used, as this is not the amount the investor would necessarily have received. Golez and Koudijs explain how, to calculate accurate returns, there must be adjustments for capital events and the currencies must be consistent. Beach et al. have also documented issues with ex-dividend and spot/forward prices.

Ethical Considerations  
This cannot be simplified to just looking at the financial return. In the Dutch descriptive history from 1739, it states that enslaved people were working the land in Banda. Nutmeg and mace were sent to officials. Whilst this is not an audited plantation record, it does show that forced labour was occurring.

The amnesty in Batavia in 1740 and a letter from the directors can be found in the Plakaatboek. These show what the official response to the violence against the Chinese was. The letter from 1742 mentions how some of the summary executions should not really be called justice, as they were more like murder. This should not be taken to mean that the directors were against all aspects of violence in the colonies.

Britain was also not innocent. The St Helena records show that in 1725 a restriction was put in place which meant that anyone freed from slavery would have to leave the island. In 1739, the records mention that nine enslaved people owned by the Company from Bombay had escaped. Some were women and children. [P05, 169, 182] This disproves the argument that the EIC was separate from slavery due to its commercial nature.

If you cannot profit from slavery or forced labour in the colonies, then neither investment would be suitable. However, if you are willing to invest in the company that might have been less harmful, the examples given still show that there were serious issues with both. We cannot reliably say which was the least harmful. The ancestor should not assume that because there was unequal documentation, there was also unequal responsibility.

Conclusion  
I would advise my ancestor to buy British stock in 1725 and sell in 1750. This is because it produces a higher percentage return, although the VOC produces a greater absolute profit in the figures used. Ethically, it would be better to invest in neither company. To give a more historically accurate answer about whether this would have been a good investment, the British assumptions would need to be changed. The real Dutch prices and whether the investor would have received the dividends would also need to be checked. This could change the recommendation.
